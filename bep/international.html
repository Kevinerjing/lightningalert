International business

Class source: 3.2 International Business

1. International trade

Know: Business transactions across national borders: creating, shipping and selling goods or services. Domestic means within a country.

Worked example: A Canadian business designs a product, has it manufactured abroad and sells it in another country. The transactions cross borders.

Try without notes: Distinguish domestic from international business.

Show suggested answer

Domestic business occurs within a country. International business involves transactions across national borders, including creation, shipping and sale of goods or services.

2. Benefits

Know: Businesses gain customers and may lower costs. Consumers gain variety and access to specialized products. Consider shipping and other costs before assuming overseas production is cheaper.

Worked example: A Canadian shop offers tropical fruit and products from countries with specialized manufacturing. A seller can reach customers outside Canada.

Try without notes: Give one business benefit, one consumer benefit and one cost to consider.

Show suggested answer

Business: access to larger markets or lower production costs. Consumer: variety or specialized quality. Cost: shipping, insurance, translation or adaptation. Overseas production is not automatically cheaper after all costs.

3. Imports, exports and balance of trade

Know: From Canada’s perspective: imports come in; exports go out for sale abroad. Balance = exports − imports. Positive: surplus. Negative: deficit. Equal: balanced trade.

Worked example: Canada exports $800B and imports $700B: $800B − $700B = +$100B, a surplus. Use the country’s perspective consistently.

Try without notes: Canada imports $680B and exports $620B. Find the balance and classify it.

Show suggested answer

620 − 680 = −$60B: a trade deficit. Imports exceed exports by $60B. Balance of trade is different from the surplus/shortage of a product in a supply–demand graph.

4. Tariff versus excise tax

Know: Tariff: tax on imports, which can raise imported prices and protect local industries. Excise: tax on a particular good or service, not necessarily imported, often passed through to consumers.

Worked example: A tax specifically on imported shoes is a tariff. A tax on gasoline sales can be an excise tax even for domestic fuel.

Try without notes: Explain one benefit and one cost of a tariff.

Show suggested answer

It can protect domestic jobs or industries by making imports more expensive. Consumers or importing businesses may face higher prices/costs. A tariff is not a tax only on domestic products.

5. Other trade barriers

Know: Exchange-rate fluctuations, differing standards or voltages, transport/logistics, storage, translation, legal costs, insurance and cultural differences.

Worked example: An exported appliance needs a different plug and voltage. A seller also pays for shipping, storage and translated instructions.

Try without notes: Classify the voltage issue and name two other barriers.

Show suggested answer

Voltage: differing standards. Other barriers include exchange-rate fluctuations, logistics, storage, translation, legal costs, insurance and cultural differences.

6. Offshore outsourcing

Know: Work performed for a business in another country, often to lower costs. Assess labour conditions, child labour risks and effects on domestic jobs rather than assuming every arrangement is unethical.

Worked example: A company hires an overseas provider to make clothes. The lower price does not reveal whether working conditions are responsible.

Try without notes: What should the company investigate before choosing the cheapest provider?

Show suggested answer

Labour conditions, safety, pay, child-labour risks, environmental practices and verification. Also assess costs and effects on domestic jobs. Offshore outsourcing is not automatically unethical.

7. Environmental degradation

Know: Resources consumed faster than nature can replenish them. Overseas demand can increase pressure on forests and other resources.

Worked example: High foreign demand drives harvesting faster than the resource can replenish itself.

Try without notes: Explain how international demand can lead to environmental degradation.

Show suggested answer

Greater demand can encourage overuse of natural resources. If consumption exceeds replenishment, resources and ecosystems deteriorate. Link the harm to the production practice, not merely the fact of trade.

8. Class examples

Know: The tariff slide describes historical 2025 events. Use it as a classroom example, not a statement of current tariff rates. No current rates are needed for these conceptual practice questions.

Worked example: The teacher’s 2025 tariff slide provides a dated case for examining trade barriers.

Try without notes: Must you memorize those rates as today’s tariff policy?

Show suggested answer

No. Use them as the dated class example. For conceptual revision, know what a tariff is, why it may be used and its effects. Current policy would need separate verification.
