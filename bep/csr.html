Class source: 3.1 CSR + Greenwashing homework

1. CSR and stakeholders

Know: Corporate Social Responsibility means managing operations responsibly and contributing to society. Stakeholders are people or groups affected by the business: workers, customers, communities, owners and others.

Worked example: A factory considers workers’ safety and the effect of its wastewater on neighbours alongside its profit.

Try without notes: Identify three stakeholders and explain why one is affected.

Show suggested answer

Workers, customers, owners and local residents are possible stakeholders. Residents are affected by pollution even if they own no shares. CSR considers business impacts beyond owners’ financial return.

2. Triple bottom line

Know: Profit: financial performance. People: fair labour, safety, truthful advertising and community wellbeing. Planet: reducing environmental harm. A strong answer considers trade-offs across all three.

Worked example: A safer, cleaner production process costs more now. It can protect workers and reduce pollution while supporting long-term customer trust.

Try without notes: Evaluate the process using all three bottom lines.

Show suggested answer

Profit: higher short-term costs and potential long-term benefits. People: safer conditions. Planet: less pollution. Name a concrete trade-off rather than claiming CSR always raises profits.

3. Sustainability

Know: Meet today’s resource needs without harming future generations’ ability to meet theirs.

Worked example: A forestry company replants and limits harvesting so forests can regenerate for future users.

Try without notes: Why is cutting trees faster than they regrow a sustainability problem?

Show suggested answer

It depletes the resource and can reduce future generations’ ability to meet their needs. Sustainable management balances present use with replenishment and future impacts.

4. Transparency and social audit

Know: Transparency means outsiders can inspect and evaluate business actions through accessible information. A social audit is an unbiased third-party assessment of social responsibility performance.

Worked example: A business publishes its labour results. An independent assessor checks its social-responsibility performance.

Try without notes: Which action is transparency and which is a social audit?

Show suggested answer

Accessible public information demonstrates transparency. An unbiased third-party assessment is a social audit. Publishing information alone does not make it an independent audit.

5. CSR business benefits

Know: Short-term costs may lead to customer loyalty, employee retention, safer workplaces, efficiency and interest from socially responsible investors (SRI). Benefits are possible, not guaranteed.

Worked example: Fair treatment can reduce staff turnover; more efficient energy use can lower waste and operating costs.

Try without notes: Give two potential business benefits of CSR and one limitation.

Show suggested answer

Possible benefits include loyalty, retention, safer workplaces and efficiency. Implementation costs money and benefits are not guaranteed or immediate.

6. ESG

Know: Environmental, Social and Governance: a structured, measurable framework. Governance concerns how a company is directed and held accountable.

Worked example: A firm tracks emissions, worker safety and board accountability using measurable indicators.

Try without notes: Match those three measures to E, S and G.

Show suggested answer

Emissions: Environmental. Worker safety: Social. Board accountability: Governance. ESG provides a more structured, measurable framework related to CSR.

7. Four greenwashing patterns

Know: Hidden trade-offs: highlights one benefit while hiding other harm. No proof: a specific claim lacks evidence. Vagueness: unclear terms such as “earth-friendly.” Irrelevance: a true claim that does not meaningfully distinguish the product.

Worked example: An ad says “earth-friendly” without defining it. Another claims 30% less waste without evidence. A third highlights packaging while hiding pollution.

Try without notes: Identify each pattern; then explain irrelevance with an example.

Show suggested answer

Undefined earth-friendly: vagueness. Unsupported specific claim: no proof. Concealed wider harm: hidden trade-offs. Irrelevance: claiming the absence of something already prohibited throughout that product category.

8. Evaluating claims

Know: Look for specific, relevant, verifiable evidence and disclosure of limitations. Attractive green packaging alone proves nothing. A credible report includes measurable results, methods and independent checks where appropriate.

Worked example: A useful water-use report states its baseline, period, measurement method, results and limitations. A leaf logo says little by itself.

Try without notes: A brand claims 40% less water use. What evidence would you ask for?

Show suggested answer

Ask: compared with what baseline, over which period, measured how, covering which operations, and verified by whom? Look for relevant data and limitations. Avoid assuming a claim is true simply because it sounds specific.
