Market structures

Class source: 2.1 Market Structures + homework

1. Perfect competition

Know: Many sellers, identical products, low entry barriers and no individual pricing power. The class tomato market is an idealized example.

Worked example: At an idealized tomato market, many sellers offer identical tomatoes at $2. A seller charging $3 loses buyers to equivalent products.

Try without notes: Why does the seller have no individual pricing power?

Show suggested answer

Buyers can switch to many sellers offering the same product. The seller takes the market price; no differentiated feature justifies a premium.

2. Monopolistic competition

Know: Many sellers with similar but differentiated products, low barriers and some pricing power. Unique features can justify a higher price.

Worked example: Two cereal brands offer similar breakfast products, but one has a special low-sugar recipe and charges more.

Try without notes: Many restaurants compete using different menus. Which structure fits, and why?

Show suggested answer

Monopolistic competition: many sellers, differentiated products, relatively low entry barriers and some pricing power. Do not confuse “monopolistic” with a single seller.

3. Oligopoly

Know: A few large sellers, high entry barriers and interdependent decisions. Products may be identical or differentiated. An oligopoly does not automatically mean firms collude; a cartel involves coordinated behaviour.

Worked example: Four airlines dominate a country. Aircraft and airport access make entry expensive. One cuts its fares; rivals consider matching the cut.

Try without notes: Give two clues for oligopoly and explain interdependence.

Show suggested answer

Few dominant sellers and high entry barriers. Each firm’s decisions can affect rivals’ sales, so rivals respond. Collusion is not required for an oligopoly.

4. Monopoly

Know: One seller, high entry barriers and high pricing power. Regulation may constrain prices. Use the local electricity network example.

Worked example: A town has one electricity-distribution network. Building a duplicate network would be costly and inefficient.

Try without notes: Does a regulated monopoly necessarily have unlimited freedom to charge any price?

Show suggested answer

No. One seller and entry barriers describe monopoly, but government regulation may limit prices and business practices.

5. Business cycle

Know: Expansion: output, employment and income grow. Peak: high point. Contraction: economic activity declines. Trough: low point before recovery. At a trough, output is low and unemployment is generally high; the slide saying unemployment is lowest is misleading.

Worked example: During recovery, businesses hire more staff and produce more. Later, activity reaches a peak and may begin to contract.

Try without notes: At a trough, would unemployment usually be low or high? Explain.

Show suggested answer

Generally high: production and demand are weak, so fewer workers are needed. The trough is the low point in economic activity, not the low point in unemployment.

Factors of production

Class source: 2.2 Factors of Production

6. Land

Know: Natural resources: trees, water, minerals, oil and farmland. Renewable resources can replenish if managed sustainably; nonrenewable resources are finite.

Worked example: A paper producer uses trees and water. A saw and a processing machine are human-made productive resources.

Try without notes: Classify a standing forest and the saw used to harvest it.

Show suggested answer

Forest: land, because it is a natural resource. Saw: capital, because it is a human-made tool used in production.

7. Labour

Know: Human work used to produce goods and services: a programmer, farmer, teacher or factory worker.

Worked example: A software developer spends five hours writing code for a customer. Their work is labour; the computer is capital.

Try without notes: A baker operates an oven. Which part is labour and which is capital?

Show suggested answer

The baker’s effort and skill are labour. The oven is capital. Classify each resource separately.

8. Capital

Know: Human-made productive resources: machinery, buildings, tools and delivery trucks. Money finances purchases; it is not itself productive capital in this lesson. Classify an item by its use.

Worked example: A van used to deliver orders is capital. A vehicle used only for personal enjoyment is a consumer good.

Try without notes: A business has $10,000 cash and purchases a commercial oven. Which is the capital factor?

Show suggested answer

The oven is productive capital. The money finances its purchase but is not itself a productive resource in the classroom definition.

9. Entrepreneurship

Know: Organizing the other resources, developing a business idea and taking risk. An owner who also codes contributes both entrepreneurship and labour.

Worked example: A student launches a weather-app business, brings resources together and accepts the risk that it may not succeed.

Try without notes: If the founder also programs the app, which two factors do they contribute?

Show suggested answer

Entrepreneurship by organizing and taking business risk; labour by doing the programming. One person can contribute more than one factor.

10. Interdependence

Know: Businesses depend on each other. A supermarket relies on farms, transport, equipment and workers; production typically combines all four resources.

Worked example: A supermarket depends on farms for food, drivers for deliveries and companies that make refrigerators.

Try without notes: Trace all four factors involved in selling a loaf of bread.

Show suggested answer

Land: wheat and water. Labour: farmers, bakers and staff. Capital: ovens, trucks and the store. Entrepreneurship: an organizer combines resources and takes risk. Interdependence links these businesses.

Market forces · Background only

The new checklist explicitly says this six-factor list does not need to be memorized. Use it as context for supply and demand; prioritize BITER and STERN.

Class source: Market Forces

11. Economic growth

Know: Income, inflation, exchange rates and interest rates influence purchasing power and business conditions.

Worked example: Higher household income may let consumers spend more on leisure activities. Inflation can reduce purchasing power if income does not keep up.

Try without notes: Why can inflation change purchasing behaviour?

Show suggested answer

When prices rise faster than income, consumers can buy less with the same budget and may reduce discretionary purchases or choose alternatives.

12. Competition

Know: Competitors’ pricing, promotion and entry into markets affect businesses.

Worked example: A rival tennis shop offers a sale. Another shop may adjust its prices, service or promotions to retain customers.

Try without notes: Name two ways competition can affect a business.

Show suggested answer

It can put pressure on prices and encourage differentiation, better service or promotion. Explain the response using the scenario.

13. Political and legal

Know: Laws, regulations and government policies affect production and marketing.

Worked example: A rule requires manufacturers to use safer packaging, changing their processes and costs.

Try without notes: A government bans a particular material. Which market force is most direct?

Show suggested answer

Political and legal: the change comes from regulation, even if businesses later develop technological responses.

14. Technology

Know: New technology can reduce production costs or replace an older product.

Worked example: A new machine produces twice as many bottles per hour. Streaming services also change demand for DVDs.

Try without notes: Can technology affect both supply and demand? Give one example of each.

Show suggested answer

Yes. Faster machinery can increase supply by lowering costs. Streaming can reduce demand for DVDs by offering a substitute.

15. Preferences and social/cultural forces

Know: Changing lifestyles, population characteristics, trends, values, beliefs and customs influence what people buy. Explain the link to the specific market.

Worked example: A community increasingly values reusable products. Shops adjust their product lines to match these preferences.

Try without notes: Explain how a change in consumer tastes can affect a reusable-bottle business.

Show suggested answer

If reusable bottles become more popular, demand shifts right. Buyers want more at each price. Preferences and social trends help explain the change.

Demand

Class source: 2.3 Law of Demand + Supply & Demand homework

16. Demand versus quantity demanded

Know: Demand is the whole price–quantity relationship. Quantity demanded is the amount buyers are willing and able to buy at one price. Ceteris paribus means all other things remain constant.

Worked example: At $20, buyers purchase 30 shirts; at $10, they purchase 60, with all other factors constant. These points belong to one demand curve.

Try without notes: What is the difference between demand and quantity demanded?

Show suggested answer

Demand describes the entire relationship between prices and quantities. Quantity demanded is the amount buyers will purchase at a specified price.

17. Own price changes

Know: A product’s own price changing causes movement along its unchanged demand curve. Lower price → greater quantity demanded. Say “quantity demanded increases,” not “demand shifts.”

Worked example: A shirt falls from $20 to $15. Buyers purchase more shirts, but their income and preferences do not change.

Try without notes: Is this an increase in demand or in quantity demanded?

Show suggested answer

Quantity demanded increases: a movement down and right along the same curve. The shirt’s own price changed; the whole demand curve did not shift.

18. BITER

Know: Buyers, Income, Tastes, Expectations, Related goods shift demand. Right means more demanded at every price; left means less at every price.

Worked example: More families move into town. At every ticket price, more buyers want tickets for a local attraction.

Try without notes: Classify the determinant and describe the demand change.

Show suggested answer

Buyers (BITER). More buyers shift market demand to the right. State the market first, then determinant and direction.

19. Normal and inferior goods

Know: Income rises: demand for a normal good rises; demand for an inferior good falls. Inferior describes the income relationship, not necessarily poor quality.

Worked example: With higher income, a household buys more restaurant meals but fewer budget instant meals. Assume these responses define the goods in the question.

Try without notes: Income rises and demand for a good falls. What type of good is it?

Show suggested answer

An inferior good. The classification describes the income–demand relationship, not a judgment that the item is defective or low quality.

20. Substitutes and complements

Know: Substitutes replace each other: Coke price rises → Pepsi demand rises. Complements are used together: gasoline price rises → SUV demand may fall. Specify which market you are analysing.

Worked example: Coke’s price rises, so buyers switch to Pepsi. Gasoline’s price rises, making SUVs more costly to operate.

Try without notes: Analyse the two markets: Pepsi and SUVs.

Show suggested answer

Pepsi demand shifts right because Coke is a substitute. SUV demand shifts left because gasoline is a complement. These are related-good price changes, not changes in the focal product’s own price.

21. Why demand slopes down

Know: Substitution effect: buyers switch to alternatives. Income effect: a higher price reduces purchasing power. Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit tends to give less additional satisfaction.

Worked example: The first slice of pizza may give more extra satisfaction than the fifth. At a higher pizza price, buyers may choose sandwiches instead.

Try without notes: Distinguish diminishing marginal utility from the substitution effect.

Show suggested answer

Diminishing marginal utility: extra satisfaction generally falls as more units are consumed. Substitution effect: buyers switch toward alternatives when the relative price rises.

Supply, equilibrium and elasticity

Class source: 2.4 Law of Supply + Supply & Demand homework

22. Supply versus quantity supplied

Know: Supply is the whole relationship. Own price rises → quantity supplied rises along the same curve, other things equal. A non-price change shifts supply.

Worked example: A phone’s own selling price increases. Producers supply more phones along their existing supply curve, all else equal.

Try without notes: Does a higher own price shift supply right?

Show suggested answer

No. It increases quantity supplied along the existing curve. A supply shift requires a non-price determinant to change.

23. STERN

Know: Subsidies & Taxes, Technology, Expectations, Resource costs, Number of sellers. Subsidy or improved technology → right. Higher production taxes or input costs → left. More sellers → right.

Worked example: A production subsidy lowers hoodie makers’ costs. Higher fabric prices raise their costs.

Try without notes: For each change, give the determinant and supply direction.

Show suggested answer

Subsidy: STERN Subsidies & Taxes, supply right. Fabric cost increase: Resource costs, supply left. At each price, production becomes respectively more or less attractive.

24. Expectations

Know: For storable goods, an expected future price rise can encourage holding stock back, decreasing current supply. An expected future price fall can encourage selling now. State the time period and assumption.

Worked example: Sellers expect a higher price next month and can store their goods, so they hold back inventory today.

Try without notes: What happens to current supply? What assumption matters?

Show suggested answer

Current supply decreases, shifting left, if sellers hold storable goods for later. Specify current supply and the storage assumption; an expectation alone does not justify every possible supply response.

25. Equilibrium

Know: Where supply and demand intersect: quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. Demand right → price up, quantity up. Demand left → both down. Supply right → price down, quantity up. Supply left → price up, quantity down.

Worked example: Initially, price is $10 and 100 units trade. More buyers enter; with supply unchanged, the new intersection has a higher price and quantity.

Try without notes: Improved production technology shifts supply right. What happens to equilibrium price and quantity?

Show suggested answer

Price falls and quantity rises, assuming demand stays unchanged. Draw S₀ and S₁ to the right, keeping the demand curve fixed.

26. Shortage and surplus

Know: At a price below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: shortage, upward price pressure. Above equilibrium: surplus, downward price pressure.

Worked example: At a price of $15, sellers offer 140 units and buyers want 80. There are 60 units of excess supply.

Try without notes: At a price of $5, buyers want 130 and sellers offer 70. Identify the gap and price pressure.

Show suggested answer

Shortage = 130 − 70 = 60 units. Price faces upward pressure. A surplus puts downward pressure on price.

27. Two curves change

Know: Demand and supply both fall: quantity falls, price is uncertain. Demand falls and supply rises: price falls, quantity is uncertain. Demand and supply both rise: quantity rises, price is uncertain. Equal price with more quantity can result from offsetting rightward shifts of both curves.

Worked example: Fewer people want pianos and stores close: demand and supply both shift left. Both changes reduce quantity, but push price in opposite directions.

Try without notes: DVD demand falls while a production tax lowers supply. What can you conclude?

Show suggested answer

Both curves shift left. Equilibrium quantity falls. Price cannot be determined without the relative sizes of the shifts. Do not assume that two left shifts must lower price.

28. Price elasticity

Know: Elastic demand responds strongly to price; inelastic demand responds weakly. Availability of substitutes matters. The class associates flatter curves with elasticity, but slope alone is not a universal measure; elasticity compares percentage changes.

Worked example: A small percentage ticket-price rise causes a large percentage fall in purchases because customers have many alternatives.

Try without notes: Is demand elastic or inelastic? Why might essentials be less responsive?

Show suggested answer

Elastic, because quantity demanded responds strongly in percentage terms. Some essentials have few substitutes, so purchases respond less strongly. Needs/wants are useful class tendencies, not universal rules.

29. GDP and the business cycle

Know: GDP measures the value of final goods and services produced within a country over a period. Rising real output often accompanies expansion; falling activity accompanies contraction. Inflation means a general rise in prices. If income does not keep up, purchasing power falls.

Worked example: Businesses produce more and hire staff during an expansion; during a contraction, sales weaken and unemployment often rises.

Try without notes: Does higher GDP mean every household is better off?

Show suggested answer

No. GDP describes overall production. Income distribution, prices and individual circumstances can differ. Distinguish real output growth from a rise caused only by higher prices.

30. Interest rates and the Bank of Canada

Know: Interest is the cost of borrowing. The Bank of Canada influences borrowing conditions through its policy interest rate. Higher rates generally discourage borrowing and spending; lower rates can encourage them. Effects take time and are not guaranteed for every business.

Worked example: Higher borrowing costs can make a factory postpone a loan-funded expansion and make households reconsider expensive purchases.

Try without notes: How can higher interest rates slow economic activity?

Show suggested answer

Loans become more costly, which can reduce consumption and investment. Weaker demand can slow growth and inflation. This is a tendency, with other factors held constant.

31. Fiscal policy

Know: Fiscal policy refers to government spending and taxation. It differs from the central bank’s interest-rate decisions. Increased government spending or lower taxes can support demand; reduced spending or higher taxes can restrain it, other things equal.

Worked example: A government funds new infrastructure; contractors hire workers and buy materials. A central bank changes its policy rate: that is monetary policy.

Try without notes: A government reduces income taxes. Is that fiscal or monetary policy? Explain a possible effect.

Show suggested answer

Fiscal policy. Households may have more disposable income and spend more, supporting demand. A Bank of Canada policy-rate change is monetary policy.
