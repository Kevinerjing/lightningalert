Detailed concepts and practice

1.1 · Business fundamentals

Class sources: Business Fundamentals slides and worksheet; Types of Businesses slides and notes.

1. Business and its purpose

Kahoot 1–2

Know: A business produces or sells goods or services to meet marketplace needs and wants, usually with the aim of earning profit for its owners. Enterprise, company and firm are related terms.

Example: A bike-repair business meets customers’ need for working bicycles and charges enough to aim for a profit.

Try: State the purpose of a business. Is revenue alone proof that it is successful?

Show suggested answer

Its purpose is to meet market demand while earning profit. Revenue is sales income; a business can earn revenue and still lose money if total costs and expenses are greater.

2. Revenue, costs, expenses and profit

Kahoot 3

Know: Revenue is money earned from sales. In this lesson, costs refer to inventory/materials or production, while expenses refer to running the business, such as rent and wages. Profit = revenue − (costs + expenses). A negative result is a loss. The checklist’s shorter “revenue − costs” uses costs to mean all costs and expenses.

Example: A stand earns $1,000, with food $400, rent $100, a permit $25 and wages $100: $1,000 − ($400 + $100 + $25 + $100) = $375 profit.

Try: A shop sells 40 items at $15 each. Materials cost $220, rent $80 and wages $150. Find revenue and profit.

Show suggested answer

Revenue = 40 × $15 = $600. Profit = $600 − ($220 + $80 + $150) = $150. Show the formula and include every listed expense exactly once.

3. Non-profit organizations

Kahoot 4

Know: A non-profit’s main purpose is a mission or community goal rather than financial gain for owners. It can collect revenue and have money left over to support that mission.

Example: A community food-support organization raises funds to provide meals.

Try: A non-profit receives $8,000 and spends $7,400. Does the $600 remaining make it a for-profit business? Explain.

Show suggested answer

No. Having a surplus does not change its primary purpose. The remaining $600 can support its mission rather than being treated as an owner’s profit.

4. Business size and SMEs

Kahoot 5

Know: Use the class employee counts: small: 1–99; medium: 100–499; large: 500+. SME means small and medium-sized enterprise. Most Canadian businesses are SMEs.

Example: A business with 99 employees is small; hiring its 100th employee makes it medium-sized under these categories.

Try: Classify businesses with 18, 100, 499 and 500 employees.

Show suggested answer

Small, medium, medium, large. Size here is measured by employees, not revenue. The slide’s background figures are 99.8% SMEs, over 50% of GDP and 64% of private-sector employment; these are class figures, not updated statistics, and the review checklist emphasizes the size ranges.

5. Goods versus services

Kahoot 6–9

Know: Goods are tangible items you can see and touch. Services are activities or assistance that satisfy needs and wants. A business may provide both.

Example: A tennis racket is a good. A tennis lesson is a service.

Try: A shop sells skates and sharpens customers’ blades. Identify its goods and services.

Show suggested answer

The skates are goods; sharpening is a service. Classify what the customer receives, not just the name of the store.

6. Service, merchandising, manufacturing and combinations

Kahoot 10–12

Know: Service businesses perform work; merchandising businesses buy finished goods and resell them; manufacturing businesses transform raw materials or parts into products. A combination performs more than one of these activities.

Example: A repair shop provides a service; a store resells ready-made helmets; a factory turns materials into helmets. The class treats restaurants as combinations, mainly service businesses.

Try: A company makes wooden desks, resells imported lamps and installs furniture. Classify each activity.

Show suggested answer

Making desks: manufacturing. Reselling lamps: merchandising. Installation: service. Overall: combination. If asked for the main type, use the activity identified as its major business interest.

7. Public versus private sector

Kahoot 13–14

Know: The public sector is government-owned or operated at federal, provincial or municipal level. The private sector is owned by private individuals or organizations. Ownership is the deciding clue.

Example: A municipally operated recreation centre is public sector. A gym owned by a family is private sector.

Try: A corporation sells shares to the public but is not government-owned. Is it in the public sector?

Show suggested answer

No. It is a publicly held corporation in the private sector. “Public corporation” refers to share ownership, while “public sector” refers to government ownership. Efficiency claims in the slides are generalizations, not definitions.

1.2 · Business ownership

Class sources: Business Ownership slides (filename starts 2.1); Characteristics of Business Ownership worksheet solution.

8. Limited versus unlimited liability

Kahoot 15–16

Know: Liability means responsibility for debts. Unlimited liability can put the owner’s personal assets at risk for business debts. Limited liability generally separates the business’s debts from owners’ personal assets; owners can still lose their investment.

Example: A sole proprietor’s personal savings may be at risk if business assets cannot cover its debts. A shareholder generally risks the amount invested.

Try: Does limited liability mean a business cannot lose money or owe debts?

Show suggested answer

No. The business can lose money and owes its debts. Limited liability limits the owners’ personal exposure; it is not a guarantee that the business or its investment is safe.

9. Sole proprietorship

Kahoot 21–22

Know: One owner; no separate legal identity from the owner; unlimited liability. Benefits: control, all profits and simple setup. Drawbacks: all responsibility, limited personal resources and no partner’s skills.

Example: One person operates an unincorporated tutoring business. Hiring employees does not create more owners.

Try: A sole proprietor hires three workers. Has it become a partnership? Give one advantage and one risk of this ownership form.

Show suggested answer

No: employees are not necessarily owners. The proprietor retains decision-making control and profits, but bears unlimited liability and the business’s responsibilities.

10. Partnership

Kahoot 17–19

Know: Two or more owners share resources and responsibilities under a partnership agreement. Benefits: complementary skills, shared workload and more capital. Drawbacks: conflict, shared profit and general partners’ unlimited liability. In a limited partnership, distinguish limited partners from the general partner.

Example: One partner handles repairs while another handles sales and bookkeeping.

Try: Explain why “every partner in a limited partnership has limited liability” is misleading.

Show suggested answer

Limited partners generally have limited liability, but a limited partnership has at least one general partner with unlimited liability. The slide’s wording is simplified; liability depends on the partner’s role. For a general partnership, general partners have unlimited liability.

11. Corporation, shareholders and directors

Kahoot 23

Know: A corporation is a separate legal entity owned through shares. Shareholders elect directors; directors oversee the business and appoint senior management. Benefits: limited liability, continuity and raising capital through shares. Drawbacks: setup cost, regulation and record keeping.

Example: A growing manufacturer issues shares to investors to finance equipment.

Try: Give two reasons incorporation might help a growing business, and one drawback.

Show suggested answer

It can raise funds by issuing shares and generally protects shareholders’ personal assets. It also continues despite changes in shareholders. A drawback is more expensive, complex administration. Explain how each feature fits the business in the question.

12. Private/public corporations, Crown corporations and privatization

Kahoot 24–26

Know: A private corporation raises equity privately; a public corporation offers shares to the public. A Crown corporation is government-owned. Privatization transfers a government-owned enterprise to private ownership.

Example: If a government sells its business to private investors, that is privatization.

Try: Does the word “corporation” tell you whether a business is government-owned?

Show suggested answer

No. A corporation is a legal structure. Check who owns it. A Crown corporation is government-owned; a public share corporation is not automatically government-owned. The teacher’s Kahoot explicitly tests private corporations, Crown corporations and privatization; treat all three as priority revision.

13. Co-operative

Kahoot 20

Know: A member-owned business serving shared needs. Core rule: one member, one vote, regardless of financial contribution. Members elect a board. Benefits include member voice and shared services/savings; drawbacks include slower decisions and disagreements.

Example: Local producers jointly own a co-op to market their products. Any patronage return typically relates to members’ use of the co-op.

Try: Two members hold different numbers of membership shares. Should the larger investor have more votes in ordinary member decisions?

Show suggested answer

No. Each member has one vote. The worksheet also marks “one vote per share” for co-operatives, which conflicts with the slides’ core membership rule. Use one member, one vote for this comparison and ask the teacher about the worksheet mark.

14. Franchisor versus franchisee

Kahoot 27

Know: A franchisor licenses a brand and business system. A franchisee pays for the right to operate using it and follows its standards. Benefits: brand recognition, training and shared advertising. Drawbacks: fees, royalties and less freedom. Franchising is an arrangement; the operator can also be a sole proprietor, partnership or corporation.

Example: An operator buys the right to open a branded sandwich shop and must use approved suppliers.

Try: Why might an experienced cook prefer an independent restaurant over a franchise? State the trade-off.

Show suggested answer

Independence allows control over the menu and suppliers and avoids franchise fees. The trade-off is building brand recognition and operating systems without the franchisor’s training and marketing support. Franchise liability depends on the operator’s legal structure.

15. Compare ownership using evidence

Checklist extension

Know: For scenario questions, identify number of owners, control, liability, capital needs and support. State a form, give evidence, and acknowledge a drawback. Business activity and ownership are different classifications.

Example: A furniture manufacturer can be a sole proprietorship or a corporation: “manufacturer” describes what it does, not who owns it.

Try: Two friends want to combine different skills and share startup costs. Recommend one possible form and identify a drawback.

Show suggested answer

A partnership fits because they can pool skills and money and divide work. In a general partnership they face unlimited liability, and disagreements are possible. Incorporation could also be defended if protection and growth are priorities; connect your answer to the evidence.

1.3 · Maslow and why we buy

Class sources: Consumer Behaviour slides; Why We Buy article and analysis questions. The checklist places these in Unit 1 despite a U2 label on the slide title.

16. Needs versus wants

Kahoot 28

Know: Needs are necessary for survival, such as basic food, water and shelter. Wants add comfort or pleasure. The underlying need and the preferred product are not always the same thing; context matters.

Example: Warm clothing is a need in an Ottawa winter; a particular luxury coat is usually a want.

Try: Explain why “food is a need, so every restaurant meal is a need” is weak reasoning.

Show suggested answer

The underlying need is nourishment. A costly restaurant meal includes choices about convenience, enjoyment or status; food could meet the need in another way. Separate the need from the particular purchase.

17. Maslow’s five levels

Kahoot 29–33

Know: Learn from bottom to top: physiological → safety → love and belonging → esteem → self-actualization. In the class model, people prioritize lower needs before higher ones. Treat it as a theory with exceptions.

Example: Food meets physiological needs; a security system supports safety; a club supports belonging; a prestige watch signals esteem; a learning experience can support personal growth.

Try: Someone buys a course to reach their creative potential, while another buys a badge to impress others. Identify each level.

Show suggested answer

The course: self-actualization, because the motive is reaching potential. The badge: esteem, because the motive is recognition or status. Classify the reason for buying; a product can satisfy different needs for different people.

18. Criticisms of Maslow

Kahoot 34

Know: The slides identify weak scientific support, an American/individualistic bias, variation in the order of needs and a non-linear path to self-actualization.

Example: An artist may pursue meaningful creative work despite insecure income or living conditions.

Try: Give two criticisms and explain one with an example.

Show suggested answer

The fixed order does not describe everyone, and the theory may prioritize individual achievement over community goals. For example, someone may sacrifice personal comfort to care for family; their priorities do not follow a strict ladder.

Six consumption influences from the slides

Distinguish the six lesson categories below from the eight article factors later.

19. Income and price

Checklist extension

Know: Income is money a person or household receives, such as wages. Higher prices can reduce what a given income can buy. Higher-income households often have more room for optional purchases.

Example: If groceries use more of an unchanged budget, a family may eat out less.

Try: If prices rise but income stays the same, must the dollar amount spent fall?

Show suggested answer

No. A household may pay more dollars for fewer goods. Its purchasing power falls; it may cut quantities, switch to cheaper brands or reduce optional purchases. Spending in dollars and quantity purchased are different.

20. Status and conspicuous consumption

Kahoot 35

Know: Status-related purchases aim for admiration or a sign of success. Conspicuous consumption means displaying purchases to impress others.

Example: Buying an expensive watch mainly so classmates notice the brand is status-driven.

Try: How is buying a coat for status different from buying it because it is fashionable?

Show suggested answer

Status is about prestige or admiration; a trend is about what is currently popular. They may overlap. Use the stated motive: impressing others points to status.

21. Current trends

Checklist extension

Know: Trends and fads shift what people consider desirable. A trend can increase demand quickly, and interest can later fade.

Example: A style of backpack becomes popular at school and sales rise.

Try: Why is it risky for a store to order too much inventory of a viral product?

Show suggested answer

Demand may fall when the trend fades, leaving unsold inventory. The business could order cautiously or monitor sales before committing to more stock.

22. Safety

Checklist extension

Know: Consumers may choose products because they trust their safety, reliability or protective features. This is especially relevant to children’s goods, equipment and appliances.

Example: A customer chooses a helmet because of its protective features rather than its colour.

Try: Identify the consumption influence and Maslow level in buying a home alarm to feel protected.

Show suggested answer

Consumption influence: safety. Maslow level: safety. A theory level and a consumption influence can have the same label but belong to different frameworks.

23. Promotion and advertising

Checklist extension

Know: Advertising, influencer endorsements and targeted promotions can attract attention and persuade people to buy.

Example: A student tries a drink after seeing an influencer promote it.

Try: A customer uses a limited-time coupon from a social media advertisement. Which influence fits?

Show suggested answer

Promotion and advertising is the clearest match. Price and timing may also contribute. For a single-best-answer question, choose the strongest clue; for an explanation, justify any additional influence.

24. Situational factors: five subtypes

Checklist extension

Know: Time: occasion, season or time of day. Surroundings: physical/social setting. Terms: payment and purchasing options. Objective: reason for the purchase. State/mood: feelings or physical state.

Example: A birthday changes timing; pleasant music changes surroundings; instalments change terms; a graduation photo creates a purpose; hunger changes state.

Try: Match: buying snacks while hungry; choosing online delivery; getting a haircut for a job interview; staying in a welcoming café; buying flowers on a birthday.

Show suggested answer

Hungry: state/mood. Delivery option: terms/how. Interview: objective/why. Café atmosphere: surroundings/where. Birthday: time/when. Multiple influences can coexist; explain the cue you used.

The article’s eight factors

These are causes of changes in spending, not types of spending. For each one, explain: change → effect on income, prices, confidence or needs → buying decision.

FactorMeaning and example
1. SeasonalityRecurring times of year change purchases: holiday gifts, summer travel. Flight bookings may be paid before hotel stays, so related spending peaks need not coincide.
2. New productsA product launch creates interest: a new game can temporarily raise entertainment spending.
3. CommutingWhere and when people work changes where they shop: office days can increase downtown lunch purchases.
4. Macroeconomic conditionsThe economy as a whole, including jobs, wages and inflation. Secure employment may encourage purchases; prices rising faster than wages reduce purchasing power.
5. Fiscal policyGovernment decisions about taxes, spending and transfers. A cash payment or tax reduction can increase households’ available money; some may save it.
6. Stock market performanceChanges in investment wealth and confidence can affect spending. Falling investments may prompt affected households to postpone optional purchases.
7. Fuel pricesHigher driving costs can reduce travel and leave less money for shopping or restaurants. Effects vary with income and dependence on driving.
8. Demographic changesChanges in population characteristics such as age, income, family size or life stage shift needs: fewer young children may reduce demand for baby products.
25. Article: jobs, inflation and fiscal policy

Kahoot 36 + checklist extension

Know: Macroeconomic conditions describe the overall economy. Fiscal policy is government action through taxation, spending or transfers. Neither term means a category of goods consumers buy.

Example: A strong job market can improve income security, while inflation can make the same pay buy less.

Try: A household receives a $300 government payment, but food prices have risen. Explain two effects on its spending.

Show suggested answer

The payment increases available funds and may support purchases or savings: fiscal policy. Higher food prices reduce purchasing power and can crowd out optional spending: inflation/macroeconomic conditions. Explain both effects; the final outcome depends on their sizes.

26. Article: events and local business decisions

Checklist extension

Know: Large events bring visitors who may spend on transport, accommodation and meals. Businesses use spending patterns to plan inventory, staffing, pricing and locations. Local data matter alongside national trends.

Example: A tournament brings families to a town, increasing demand near the arenas. This is an original example of the same mechanism as the article’s concert example.

Try: Why could a café near an arena gain more than a shop far away? Suggest one business response.

Show suggested answer

Visitors need nearby food and convenience, so location affects who benefits. The café could schedule extra staff and prepare more stock for peak times. The same event will not increase every business’s sales equally.

27. Article: demographics and different responses

Kahoot 37 + checklist extension

Know: Different groups and products respond differently to price and income changes. Necessities are often harder to cut than optional spending; substitutes, income and purpose matter. Private ownership does not shield a business from economic changes.

Example: A low-income commuter may cut restaurant meals when fuel becomes more expensive; a high-income household may keep its usual habits.

Try: Do all goods and services respond the same way to higher prices or lower incomes? Give a reason and a contrast.

Show suggested answer

No. People still need basic groceries, but may postpone a luxury holiday. Necessity and substitutes affect how much they cut back. Income also changes how strongly a price rise affects a budget. Avoid saying private businesses are unaffected by the wider economy.

Clarifications to avoid memorizing contradictions
  • Co-op voting: the solution sheet checks both voting descriptions; the slides and Canadian reference support one member, one vote for member governance.
  • Co-op financing: the slide’s “only” new members/sales wording is too narrow. Co-ops may also use investment shares and loans. Equal votes do not necessarily mean equal financial contributions.
  • Limited partnerships: the slide’s sentence about “each partner” needs the general/limited partner distinction.
  • Franchises: continuity and personal liability depend on the operating legal entity and agreement; a franchise label alone does not guarantee either.
  • Private corporations: the slide and Kahoot explicitly use the 50-shareholder description. Recognize private corporation as the expected class answer, while remembering that this number is a classroom simplification rather than a universal rule.
  • Franchise payments: the slides refer to part of profits; in practice fees depend on the contract and may be based on sales. The practice calculations explicitly state their basis.
  • Disposable income: the slide uses this loosely for optional spending. More precisely it means after-tax income; money left after necessities is discretionary income.
  • Maslow: the fixed ladder is the model taught; its criticisms are explicitly examinable. Product choice depends on motive.

Where a marked worksheet contradicts the lesson, ask the teacher which wording is intended; do not silently memorize both.
