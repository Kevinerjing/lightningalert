THE STORY / ABOUT THE NOVEL

One astronaut. A planet of problems.

The Martian by Andy Weir follows Mark Watney, an astronaut and botanist with mechanical engineering expertise, stranded on Mars. Every day becomes an experiment in chemistry, biology, physics and engineering.

This field guide explores the scientific ideas behind his survival — and separates realistic engineering from fictional drama.

01 / 19🌱

Growing Potatoes

Potatoes are living stem tubers. Their 'eyes' can produce shoots. With light, water, carbon dioxide, nutrients and suitable temperature, plants grow through photosynthesis.

Martian regolith is not garden soil: it has little organic matter and may contain toxic perchlorates.

Plausible concept, major practical difficulties
02 / 19💧

Making Water

The film uses hydrazine rocket fuel as a source of hydrogen. Hydrogen then reacts with oxygen: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. This is both a synthesis reaction and a combustion reaction.

Forming water releases a great deal of heat; a premixed hydrogen–oxygen gas can explode.

Correct chemistry, hazardous execution
03 / 19🛰️

The Airlock Explosion

The habitat must be pressurized because Mars has less than 1% of Earth's surface air pressure. When the airlock breaks, gas rushes outward from high to low pressure.

The potato crop loses its protective environment, facing depressurization, cold and water loss.

Physically grounded, dramatized details
04 / 19🌪️

The Martian Dust Storm

Dust storms occur across Mars, and some can cover the entire planet. Yet the atmosphere is extremely thin, so even high-speed winds push much less strongly than similar winds on Earth.

Dust blocking sunlight and covering solar panels is a more serious danger than knocking over massive equipment.

Opening sequence greatly exaggerated
05 / 19☀️

Solar Power on Mars

Solar panels turn sunlight into electrical energy. On average, Mars gets about 43% of the sunlight per unit area that Earth gets, because Mars is farther from the Sun.

Dust accumulation and seasonal changes reduce the energy supply further.

Real mission engineering
06 / 19📡

Talking with Earth

Watney finds the old Pathfinder spacecraft and uses it to communicate. Radio waves move at the speed of light, but Earth–Mars signals can take several to more than twenty minutes one-way.

Long delays make real-time conversations impossible; messages must be sent asynchronously.

Based on real spacecraft communications
07 / 19🪐

The Gravity Assist

A spacecraft can pass near a moving planet and exchange orbital energy and momentum with it. Its speed and direction relative to the Sun can change without using as much fuel.

Gravity assists are used by real interplanetary spacecraft.

Real orbital mechanics
08 / 19🚀

The Final Rescue

When a jet of gas exits a spacesuit, momentum conservation pushes the astronaut the opposite way. This illustrates Newton's third law: forces occur in equal and opposite pairs.

A punctured spacesuit is a dangerous, imprecise way to steer in space.

Correct principle, unrealistic control
09 / 19🕒

Time on Mars: Sol vs. Earth Day

A sol is one Martian solar day: about 24 hours, 39 minutes and 35 seconds. That is approximately 1.02749 Earth days, or 2.75% longer than a day on Earth.

In The Martian, Watney counts his mission in sols because life on Mars follows the Martian sunrise and sunset. A Martian year is about 668.6 sols (687 Earth days).

02 / THINK DEEPER

Three big questions.

Select a question to reveal its scientific explanation.

01Why can burning hydrogen make water and still cause an explosion? ＋

Hydrogen and oxygen react to create water: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Breaking old bonds absorbs energy, but forming strong O–H bonds releases even more. The net reaction releases heat. If a mixed gas ignites very rapidly, it heats and expands suddenly, causing a pressure wave. The final product can be ordinary water even though getting there is dangerous.

02Why did Watney's potatoes die after the airlock failed? ＋

Plants require more than just light. The airlock breach destroyed the habitat's controlled pressure and temperature. In the cold, low-pressure Martian environment, liquid water becomes unstable, plant cells lose water and may freeze, and tissues are damaged. Restoring the habitat later cannot necessarily bring severely damaged plants back to life.

03Why does a fast wind on Mars push less forcefully than the same wind on Earth? ＋

A useful model is dynamic pressure: q = ½ρv², where ρ is gas density and v is wind speed. Mars has an exceptionally low atmospheric density, so at the same speed its wind carries far less momentum per unit volume. Martian storms can still be serious because they block sunlight and cover equipment in dust.

03 / BEYOND THE FILM

Ten deeper science challenges.

Explore the physics, chemistry, planetary science and systems engineering described in Andy Weir's novel. Each concept includes a deeper question and answer.

10 / 19☢️

The RTG Heater

Watney retrieves a radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG) containing plutonium-238. Radioactive decay produces heat; thermoelectric elements can convert some of it to electricity. In the novel, the device's heat helps warm his rover.

Think deeper: Why does an RTG stay warm for decades?

Answer: Pu-238 has a half-life of about 87.7 years, so its decay—and heat output—decreases slowly. It is not a nuclear chain-reaction reactor.
11 / 19🫧

Making Oxygen from CO₂

Mars's atmosphere is roughly 95% carbon dioxide. Oxygen production requires energy to separate oxygen atoms from carbon-containing molecules. NASA's MOXIE experiment demonstrated oxygen extraction from Martian CO₂.

Think deeper: Can astronauts breathe Martian air directly?

Answer: No. The air is far too thin and mostly CO₂. An oxygen-generation system and a pressurized habitat are essential.
12 / 19🛞

Rover Traction in Low Gravity

Mars's gravity is about 3.71 m/s², only 38% of Earth's. On level ground, a simplified traction limit is F ≤ μN, with N ≈ mg. Reduced weight can lower grip, although less weight also reduces the force needed to accelerate the rover.

Think deeper: Is driving easier in low gravity?

Answer: Not always: reduced normal force can reduce available tire traction, and loose soil, slope and wheel design all matter.
13 / 19🛡️

Radiation on Mars

Mars has no strong global magnetic field and little atmospheric shielding. Astronauts are exposed to galactic cosmic rays and potentially intense solar energetic-particle events. Shielding, storm shelters and dose monitoring are critical.

Think deeper: How can a Mars habitat reduce radiation exposure?

Answer: Water, supplies and regolith can provide shielding; solar storms may require a specially shielded shelter. No practical shield removes all cosmic-ray risk.
14 / 19🌌

Earth–Mars Transfer Orbits

Spacecraft aim for where Mars will be, not where it is at launch. A Hohmann transfer is an idealized elliptical orbit between two circular, coplanar orbits. Real missions optimize trajectories for fuel, timing and arrival conditions.

Think deeper: Why are Mars launch windows spaced roughly 26 months apart?

Answer: Earth and Mars must reach favorable relative positions. Their synodic period is about 780 Earth days, or 26 months.
15 / 19♻️

Closed-Loop Life Support

A crew needs breathable air, drinkable water, food, temperature control and waste processing. Recovering water and regenerating oxygen can reduce resupply needs, but real systems have losses and require maintenance.

Think deeper: Could a habitat recycle absolutely everything?

Answer: Not with present-day technology. Even highly regenerative systems need energy, replacement parts and some makeup supplies.
16 / 19🚀

The Rocket Equation

Watney's ascent vehicle must gain enough speed to meet the rescue spacecraft. The ideal rocket equation is Δv = vₑ ln(m₀/mf), where vₑ is effective exhaust velocity, m₀ initial mass and mf final mass.

Think deeper: Why does removing mass help a rocket?

Answer: With the same propellant and exhaust speed, lower final mass increases the mass ratio and attainable Δv. Structural strength and safety still impose limits.
17 / 19🔋

A Rover's Energy Budget

An electric rover must balance solar energy collected, battery capacity, traction energy, heating and other electrical loads. A simple estimate is E = P × t, with watt-hours or kilowatt-hours as energy units.

Think deeper: How much energy does an 800 W rover use in six hours?

Answer: E = 800 W × 6 h = 4,800 Wh = 4.8 kWh. This simplified result ignores variable loads and charging losses.
18 / 19🧭

Navigation Without GPS

Mars has no Earth-style GPS constellation. Rover navigation can combine orbital maps, landmarks, inertial measurements, cameras, wheel odometry and the Sun's position. Dust, dunes and wheel slip make route planning difficult.

Think deeper: How can a rover tell whether its wheels are slipping?

Answer: It can compare wheel-rotation estimates with visual motion measured by cameras or inertial sensors; disagreements indicate possible slip.
19 / 19🧰

Engineering Redundancy

Space systems use backups, fault detection and contingency procedures because repairs and resupply are difficult. A backup must avoid sharing the same single point of failure as the primary system.

Think deeper: Why isn't one highly reliable component enough?

Answer: Even a component with low failure probability can fail. Independent redundancy, testing and safe fallback modes reduce mission risk, though they add mass and complexity.

SCIENCE & CAREERS

Was Mark Watney an Environmental Engineer?

Not exactly. Mark Watney is the Ares 3 mission's botanist and has mechanical engineering expertise. But many of his survival challenges overlap with environmental engineering: water recovery, waste reuse, controlled-environment agriculture, air management and resource sustainability. Environmental engineering combines chemistry, biology, physics and engineering to solve problems like these.

🌱01 / 08

How a Potato Sprouts

Potatoes are stem tubers. Their eyes contain buds that can produce shoots and roots, creating a genetically similar plant without fertilization.

Why can a piece of potato become a new plant?

A tuber stores energy and contains living buds with cells that divide and differentiate into new tissues.

☀️02 / 08

Turning Light into Food

In chloroplasts, plants use light energy to produce sugars from carbon dioxide and water. Overall: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.

What happens when Mars provides less sunlight?

Photosynthesis may slow, limiting growth unless light, temperature, CO₂ and nutrients are managed.

🧫03 / 08

What Happens When Plants Freeze?

Plant cells have cell walls, membranes and large vacuoles. Rapid depressurization, dehydration and freezing can damage membranes and tissues.

Why might the plants not recover after the airlock is repaired?

Severe membrane damage and ice-related injury can kill cells; restoring pressure cannot reverse dead tissue.

🦠04 / 08

Microbes and Martian Soil

Microorganisms can break down organic matter and help cycle nutrients. Martian regolith lacks a developed soil ecosystem, and untreated human waste can contain pathogens.

Can potatoes grow without soil microbes?

Yes, in controlled hydroponic systems with the right dissolved nutrients; microbes are useful but not strictly required.

💧05 / 08

How Roots Take Up Water

Water crosses selectively permeable cell membranes through osmosis. Healthy plant cells use water pressure inside the vacuole to maintain turgor.

Why do leaves wilt when plants lose water?

As cells lose water, turgor pressure falls and the plant becomes less rigid.

⚡06 / 08

Turning Potatoes into ATP

Human cells release usable energy from nutrients through cellular respiration. A simplified aerobic equation is C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy.

Why does Watney need oxygen if he has food?

Oxygen enables efficient aerobic ATP production in mitochondria. Food alone cannot sustain that process.

🥔07 / 08

Calories Are Not Everything

Potatoes provide carbohydrates, potassium and some vitamin C, but a potato-only diet lacks adequate sources of several essential nutrients, including vitamin B12 and essential fatty acids.

Can calories alone guarantee survival?

No. The body also needs adequate protein, essential fats, vitamins, minerals and water.

♻️08 / 08

A Habitat as an Ecosystem

Plants take up CO₂, water and nutrients; astronauts consume food and oxygen and release CO₂ and waste. Recycling can reduce resupply needs, but real habitats require energy and maintenance.

Can a few plants supply all the oxygen for one astronaut?

Not automatically. Oxygen production depends on illuminated leaf area, growth conditions and respiration by both plants and people.
